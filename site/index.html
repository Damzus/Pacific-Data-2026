The island stays.
The village goes.

Saurab NandMohammed Junaid HanifKaran Parmar

“The Pacific is sinking” is one sentence stretched over thousands of islands that are doing opposite things. Twenty-five years of satellite shoreline measurements say that in Choiseul five islands vanished completely — while in the Carterets, whose people are being evacuated, the shoreline is gaining land. Land area and habitability are not the same measurement.

Whether people can stay is not decided by how much land there is. It is decided by whether the wells are fresh, the gardens grow, and the sea stays out of the house.

The same ocean. Nine different answers.

Sea level is rising at every one of these sites and the ocean is warmer at all of them. That forcing is regional and near-uniform — the spread is 3.8 to 5.4 millimetres a year. The shoreline response is not: it runs from −0.17 to +0.44 metres a year, and it is not predicted by how fast the sea is rising (Pearson r = +0.13, p = 0.74). Rows are in the same order in both charts — if sea level set the outcome, the right-hand chart would echo the left.

How far it moved vs. where it ended up

The grey bar is the full range each shoreline swept across 25 years. The dot is where it actually finished. Movement is large everywhere and the net result is small — Kayangel swept almost 41 metres to end half a metre from where it began. These coasts are not still; they are churning in place.
